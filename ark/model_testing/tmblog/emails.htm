(JHK) The proper term is broach. "Caught in a trough" probably reflects the propensity for oceanographers to use four words where one will do . Actually, wind can contribute to broaching. Remembering that most broaches occur in following seas, if the wind is coming from the same direction, it can contribute force, especially if the house is aft. The opposite is true for anchored ships. I used this extensively in an effort several years ago. But, the dominant force in broaching is the sea, due to the much higher density of water over air.

There are rules for rudder size and steering gear power. However, in broaching incidences, estimating the disturbing force is very difficult. First, most of these actions happen in following seas. Therefore, the encounter frequencies are very low. Conventional predictive codes, such as strip theory, do not work. It is also a dynamic event, and the forces on the hull are constantly changing. That's why broaching is best studied in the model basin. Actually, the rudder and steering gear are designed relatively late in the process because they have relatively little impact on ship size, arrangements, cost, etc. (James 3:4)

The photo is interesting because it appears that the ship is underway. Most broaching incidences on sizable ships occur when they have lost power. (There is a long history of fishing vessels broaching under power in very rough seas.) This ship should have been able to get out of the trough. Perhaps this ship lost its rudder.
